## 1. The product

A person types their X handle and one sentence about what they cover. Oparax reads their recent X activity once to learn what they actually follow, recommends at most ten websites and feeds that publish it, and builds them a page at oparax.ai/<handle>. From then on it watches those sources, judges every new item against their beat, folds items about one story into one card in English, fills the page, and alerts them in their X DMs through the Oparax bot. It recommends at least five X accounts and watches the ones the person picks, within a monthly pool of posts. Later, a person can add a daily digest of GitHub repos and Product Hunt launches that fit.

In the owner's words (September 16 and 17): "You already live on X, you already see X. What about the wide internet that you don't see on X? Oparax brings it to you." and "I'll monitor the internet, GitHub and Product Hunt for you outside of X and give you that info on X."

X is used for three things: learning what the person cares about, reaching them, and watching the X accounts they choose, within a monthly allowance of posts. Drafting, voice guides and posting are gone (owner, August 26 and September 14).

Who it is for, as recorded: people who follow a beat and publish about it. AI content creators first (owner, August 27), and among them the creator still building an audience is the one the GitHub and Product Hunt digest is a bet on (owner, September 19); reporters second; Reshad, the football reporter, is the test on a different beat (the owner, September 10: "Reshad gets 95 percent of his news from articles, not X posts"). The first cohort is five people the owner knows: Farzan, Kush, Liam, Nihan, Reshad. The owner builds their monitors himself and sends each a link.

The diagnose verdict of September 16 still governs pace: no one has reacted to monitoring output yet and no one has paid, so the first slice goes to the five and the rest follows what they do, not what we guess.

## 3. Onboarding

The code in `lib/onboarding/` is the specification (owner, September 27: "the code is the spec"; the same evening: "I've overcomplicated for no damn reason"). Three steps. One: code looks the person up on X (profile, picture, and the pinned post with its pictures and quoted post; the location is not given to the model). Two: code reads their 10 newest own posts of the past 3 months, a thread counting as one post, a photo as the photo and a video or GIF as its freeze frame. Three: Jev scores every row of the shared table and every account the person quotes (a mention is not enough) in one request, code keeps those at 0.35 or above, and one GPT-6 Luna fast call through the AI Gateway reads the beat, the profile, the posts and what passed and recommends up to ten sites and feeds in total (a feed wins a close call; a stream that repeats another pick is skipped) and at least five X accounts, each with its score and one sentence of why; when fewer than five accounts fit, it names terms for one X search for accounts, code runs it, Jev scores the authors, and a second call finishes. No web search, no link reading, no PDF reading, and no new source is checked or written into the table. The former walkthrough streamed to a bare `/onboarding` page and stopped after step one. That historical switch is absent from current source: 151 uses authenticated setup at `/onboarding`, prepares the monitor in the background and shows progress on its per-handle page. Measured September 27: Liam, 24 seconds, $0.278 ([references/cogs.md](references/cogs.md)). The rest of this section is the September 19 design, kept as history and superseded by this paragraph where they differ.

Settled September 19. Four steps: read the person (Grok fetches fixed X searches, code pulls links, accounts and hashtags from the posts); rank every known source against them (Jev, in code, under a second, under a cent; strong at 0.75, possible at 0.35, below that dropped); Grok picks at most ten for the page, preferring what the person's own activity points to, and searches the web only for parts of the beat nothing covers; the page. New sources it finds are checked, given a written description and added to the shared table for the next person. Measured: 8 cents and a minute for Reshad, 26 cents and four minutes for Liam, against about $1 each on September 15 (on grok-4.6; at grok-4.7's price, the model since September 21, the same runs come to about 5 and 16 cents, see [references/cogs.md](references/cogs.md)).

The shared table is the asset: one row per recurring stream, read by a person as "Mundo Deportivo · FC Barcelona" with a plain-English description of who publishes it and what that stream actually posts, plus its language, how we fetch it, and how often it publishes. 76 verified rows seed it ([source-table-seed.json](source-table-seed.json)). We do not crawl sites or index the web: a publisher's own feed or section page is already the index of its new content, a stream the table lacks was found during onboarding until September 27 (a rented web search, then the model naming streams and code checking them) and remembered by the table; since the September 27 cut onboarding only recommends from the table, so the table grows only when rows are added to it.

The prompt is `lib/onboarding/prompts.ts`. The September 19 costs and the twenty-odd directions tried and dropped by then are in the September 19 onboarding spec (deleted September 27; git 1e9e0bc); the main rejected directions are also lines in [references/decisions.md](references/decisions.md).

## 4. The feed: judging, grouping, cards

What exists from the drafting product and carries forward: a Qwen stage that judges one item on or off the beat with a reason, and a Qwen stage that writes an English headline and fact lines from an item in any language (measured in August at $0.000116 and $0.00025 a call). Each item is its own card today; grouping was built on July 23 and removed the next day, and a single merged judge-and-write call was tried on July 26 and split back on August 10 because its output broke deliveries.

The owner's direction (September 17 and 18): judging and grouping are redesigned together as one algorithm and that is its own slice. Every incoming item passes through Jev first for whether it fits what the person has said they want; for grouping, Jev gives a probability that the item is the same news as a recent story; Qwen reads that, decides only whether the item adds anything, and writes. The person's corrections, per source or per story, are compiled into what Jev reads, so its judgments follow them. Jev keeps nothing between calls; what grows is the facts we send it. It enters behind a test on real items against the owner's own labels, half of them Spanish, with Qwen as the fallback whenever Jev is unsure or unreachable and always as the writer. A first quick check (September 19, 24 items, under a twentieth of a cent): Jev judged real Catalan and Spanish headlines and X-style posts in their own language as well as their English translations (23 of 23 on the right side against a narrow FC Barcelona beat either way, average score difference 0.05), and recognized a Catalan article and a Spanish article as the same story at 0.95. So nothing is translated before Jev. Two items scored 0.23 in Catalan against 0.03 in English, but the English had been given extra words by the translator ("basketball", "women's team") that the original never said, so that gap is the test's, not Jev's. This does not replace the test against the owner's labels.

The card (decided August 28): a synthesized headline, one to five fact lines each with its source, the contributing publishers, one compact relative time, an image only when a source had one.

Settled September 28 (owner's one yes on the reconciliation): day zero is each source's 10 newest items from the last 2 days (first said September 26); the fit line is 0.5, with the unsure band counted off; the writer is Qwen 3.7 Flash with Jev doing the checks; grouping is in this build, articles only at first ("that stays. Obviously, that's a part of this"), with the assistant's 72-hour story window and 0.75 join and adds lines recorded as defaults. The exact rules are [downstream-algorithm.md](downstream-algorithm.md). Still open: how a card changes when an item joins (append and deduplicate is the proposal) and whether a skipped item can be flipped back.

## 5. Keeping it moving

Ruled September 28 (owner's yes to the assistant's recommendation): watched X accounts are polled from a Vercel cron, every minute for Wire and every five minutes for the other tiers, with replies and reposts excluded so they are never billed; the Activity API serves only the bot's incoming DMs. Feeds and sections are polled often with conditional requests, which cost nothing, and each new item is judged as it arrives. The owner's words (September 16): "cant we poll my sites and RSS literally every minute?"; one to five minutes is the assistant's range. Recommended by the September 17 stack walk for sites and feeds, not separately confirmed: the same Vercel cron (once a minute is available on the plan already paid for), Railway has no remaining role, and the old X stream worker is retired. The retained foundation is `lib/sources/discovery.ts`, the feed and sitemap parsers, and `lib/sources/article-text.ts`; the retired conditional-fetch and Bright Data code is preserved at `archive/legacy-drafting` for reference. The old poller was only safe because exactly one copy ran; on a scheduler two runs can overlap, so a claim taken at the start of each run is required before anything is delivered. Poll each unique source once for everyone who watches it. Full article text is what the judging and card-writing stages read; a feed's own one-line teaser is not enough to write one to five fact lines from, and only about 6 of the 76 seed feeds carry the whole article inside the feed. So every new item's page is fetched. Measured September 19, free: fetching a recent article directly, with no Bright Data, gave readable full text for 66 of the 76 seed sources; the other 10 are all section pages where the quick test script picked the wrong links, not confirmed walls, and all 10 passed the product's real checker on September 18. One publisher (Sport) refuses a request that identifies itself as a bot and accepts a normal browser identity. Decided (owner, September 19): every fetch presents itself as a normal browser; the old poller's rule of announcing itself as Oparax's bot was written by an earlier assistant session, not by him, and is dropped. The walled sources met on September 18 (OpenAI's news page, Meta's AI blog, MarkTechPost's feed, AI News's feed and one more) are already kept out of the table, and OpenAI is covered by its feed, which reads fine. The database holds no history of which old fetches needed Bright Data (it was only in the deleted Railway project's logs). The owner's words, September 19: he is "wondering if we can do away with brightdata" to reduce complexity "but not at the cost of sacrificing monitoring". The assistant's recommendation on this evidence is that it can go: the first build fetches directly, a source that cannot be read directly is not admitted (already the rule), the checker's last-verified date catches a source that later puts up a wall, and Bright Data comes back only if a source people actually want turns out to need it (it costs $0.0015 a fetch). Do not keep article bodies past the window in which a story can still grow; keep the card. Expire already-seen keys after a month or two. The daily spend watchdog, built after one source looped for three days in August and cost $69, gets its own schedule.

## 6. GitHub and Product Hunt

Decided September 19 (owner: "that defines the product"). Not part of onboarding, which recommends sites and feeds only, and not blocking anything (owner, the same day): a later addition that a person adds to a running monitor by hand. A daily digest, separate from the ten sites, for people whose beat is tools and launches: new and fast-rising repos in their interest areas, the day's Product Hunt launches that fit, each as a card with the name, a why-now line and two lines on what it does; a weekly pass of large established repos they have not covered; and releases of repos they have covered. Interest areas come from the beat and posts, numbers qualify candidates in code for free, and the beat judgment makes it personal. The mechanics and the evidence behind it are in section 8 of the September 19 onboarding spec (deleted September 27; git 1e9e0bc). It is a bet: Liam said GitHub would be "useful eventually", runs a weekly repo series by hand, and never mentions Product Hunt; Nihan touches both only in paid posts. The test is whether anything either of them posts came from the digest. Before any paying customer sees Product Hunt data, its terms require an email to them about commercial use. Ruled September 28 (owner): the digests are optional switches a signed-up person turns on after onboarding, built in the one feature; the Product Hunt email goes out at the first paying user and is not raised again.

## 7. The bot

The bot is `@oparax_ai`, in the developer console with messaging permissions and registered chat keys; `@oparax` is taken and no separate brand account is made (owner, September 24). Nothing in the code hard-codes a handle. Nothing has ever been sent or received. Verified from X's documentation on September 17: sending a DM costs $0.015; a received DM event costs $0.010; 1,440 sends per app per day and 15 per fifteen minutes per person; incoming messages arrive through the X Activity API by a webhook that answers X's challenge and verifies X's signature; one subscription on the bot's own account covers every monitor; nothing in X's rules requires the person to message first. Recommended and not yet confirmed: send through the plain DM endpoints with `@oparax_ai` connected as an ordinary account, because the registered bot keys have never been loaded at runtime and the price is documented only for the plain path. Activation: a button on the page opens the person's own composer addressed to the bot with a one-time code; sending it links the DM to the monitor. Replies: STOP, PAUSE and RESUME as fixed words, everything else logged for the owner. The bot carries news only (owner, September 28); there is no day-seven payment message, the page shows the prices instead.

The cost dial: at $0.015 a send, alerting every card is $7 to $22 a day on a busy monitor. The owner's point, September 19: alerts follow what the person asks for, so a person who wants every update the moment it lands costs several times what a once-a-day person costs, and the price has to carry that; it is not a fixed few a day. One DM can carry several stories for the same $0.015, so how alerts are batched moves this bill more than anything else. Ruled September 28 (owner): one DM per story, ever. Every report of one story is clustered into one story; the person gets one DM for it, carrying the link to that exact story on their page (oparax.ai/<handle>/<story id> opens the feed with the card in view; "whatever cluster story is being sent, linked to that"); later reports improve the card there and never DM again ("the news is that Ronaldo gets transferred to Juventus. If all other places bring that news in, it gets clustered into one story, but it's not alerting the user again on the bot DM. Better yet, the bot can, in the DM, along with the news, also have a link to the exact clustered story on our website"). Cadence by tier: Hobby and Creator once a day (Creator every 2 hours later if asked; the daily alert was first set September 19); Wire "every 15 minutes giving a sort of mini digest, or nothing if there's no news." No instant alerts. The arithmetic per cadence is in [references/cogs.md](references/cogs.md) section 3: a daily alert is 45 cents a month, Wire at full pace $43.20; X's 1,440 sends a day per app means about 15 Wire users at full pace fill the sender. How the stories are packed into one DM is the assistant's to design in the build. The second judgment on whether a story is worth a DM remains an assistant proposal the owner has not approved. Prerequisites: the X API balance was topped up with $6 on September 19 (about $5.85 left after that day's count test) and calls fail whenever it runs out; one real send proves the path.

## 9. Payment

Day seven, Stripe hosted checkout on the direct Stripe account the owner created on September 24 (its Oparax sandbox keys are in Vercel; going live swaps in live keys), three prices (the tiers below), no Stripe-side trial because our clock is the trial, the customer portal for cancelling, refunds by hand. Checkout is the sign-up (owner, September 28): the checkout email creates or links the account and binds the page. Four events matter: first payment, renewal paid, payment failed, subscription ended; each updates paid-through and status, and the webhook must verify Stripe's signature against the unparsed request. Nothing is built.

Ruled September 28 (owner): three tiers. Hobby $5 a month (100 watched X posts a month, a daily alert); Creator $30 (3,000 posts, a daily alert); Wire $99 (4,000 posts, a mini digest every 15 minutes when there is news). Sites and feeds are unlimited on every tier. The $5 and $30 are his ("I want to price my startup tier at $5. The creator tier can be at $30"); the $99 and the three pools are the assistant's recommendation he accepted ("I'm good with either. What do you recommend?"). A fourth tier waits for a first payer. The free week carries 300 watched posts and asks for no card. The monthly pool is the only allowance number: no per-account limits; the picker shows each account's posts per day (one counts request, cached) and a running total; one warning when an account would empty the pool in under 10 days; at zero the person's accounts pause until the month resets, sites continue, and the page says so. Margins at full use, from [references/cogs.md](references/cogs.md) section 6: Hobby about 50%, Creator about 37%, Wire about 30% in the worst case and about 52% on a normal week. Every cost, with its source and the arithmetic per person, lives there: fetching is free, judging and writing are well under a dollar to a few dollars a month by volume, a daily alert is 45 cents a month, onboarding is under 50 cents once, and watched X posts are half a cent each. Anchors before the ruling: Reshad said $4 a month, Brieflet charges $12 for similar personalization, the owner floated $5, $20 and $100 in August.

Decided September 19: watched X accounts are gated by volume, not by a count of accounts. A plan carries a monthly pool of watched posts (a pool rather than a daily cap, so one busy day does not switch the monitor off at 2 pm), and each account shows its usual posts per day when it is picked so the person can see what it will use. Websites, feeds, GitHub and Product Hunt are not metered: fetching them is free (GitHub's API and Product Hunt's API charge nothing; their limits are request rates, and a source is fetched once for everyone who watches it), and what they cost us is judging and writing at a fraction of a cent an item. The owner's working pitch (about 100 watched posts a day, 3,000 a month, everything else unlimited, about $30) became the Creator tier on September 28. Why volume: X charges half a cent per delivered post, so the bill follows how much the accounts post, not how many there are: five company or founder accounts are about $5 to $10 a month; five transfer journalists and outlets are $22 to $45; 3,000 posts is $15. A watched account is billed once however many people watch it, so cost falls where users overlap, and football is where everyone watches the same ten people.

Replies and reposts, measured September 19 with X's counts endpoint over seven days (about 12 cents): who the account is decides everything. A founder who argues in the replies (levelsio) posted 345 times, of which 275 were replies and 11 reposts, so watching him raw is 49 posts a day and filtered is 8, six times cheaper. A transfer journalist (Fabrizio Romano, 241 posts, 5 replies, 2 reposts) and an outlet (Mundo Deportivo, 877 posts, 17 and 42) are almost all original posts, so filtering saves them 3 to 7 percent and nothing more; Mundo Deportivo alone is 117 posts a day, more than a whole 100-a-day pool. Company and lab accounts (OpenAI, Anthropic, Sam Altman) posted about 6 times in the week. What X's documentation says about receiving them: on the Activity API a watched account's replies, quotes and reposts all arrive as posts, each is billed as a post, and a subscription has no filter to leave them out. The filtered stream does have one: a rule such as `from:handle -is:retweet -is:reply` stops them before delivery, so they are never billed; pay-per-use allows 1,000 rules of 1,024 characters each on one connection, which is tens of thousands of handles. The catch is that the stream needs one always-on connection, which is what the old Railway stream worker was; that Railway project has been deleted, so the worker's code in `ingest/` would have to be hosted again somewhere. Superseded September 28 (owner's yes to the assistant's polling recommendation, section 5): watched accounts are polled from a Vercel cron with replies and reposts excluded, no stream worker is hosted, and the Activity API stays for the bot's DMs only.

How the price was reasoned about (assistant's account, September 19; the tiers were ruled September 28). Cost sets the floor and nothing else: at full use a $30 plan costs about $15 for X, $1 to $2 for judging and writing, and alerts on top, so alerts are what can sink it. The price itself comes from what the person already pays for the same job and what the job earns them, not from a multiple of cost. A creator who sells sponsored posts is buying material for posts that earn money; tools in that position (schedulers, growth tools, research feeds) sit between roughly $20 and $100 a month, while a reader who just wants to keep up (Reshad, $4) is a different customer at a different price. So the experiment starts at the top of what is believable for creators and comes down, because the first users are recruited by hand, a price can always be lowered for one person and never quietly raised, and the objection heard at a high price is the most useful thing the experiment can collect. Tiers exist to anchor: a wire tier (every update, instantly) priced two to three times the middle one makes the middle one the obvious choice and carries its own alert bill.

## 13. Build order

One feature (owner, September 28: "I am done planning ... I want to trigger a feature such that everything gets developed ... literally every single thing"). ## 15. How it got here

- **Through September 11.** The drafting product shipped through August; customer discovery (178 reporters and 23 creators contacted, two demos, no activations) is in findings.md. Experiment 1 was designed September 11 around personalized news delivery; production was paused and the workers deleted on September 12.
- **September 12 to 15.** Four generations of source discovery, about $20 of model spend. What survived: Perplexity over Grok's own web search; fixed X reads that Grok executes literally; a shared table of accepted sources; the rule that a page and its feed are one stream. What the owner cut, each time asking who had introduced it: a summary step, English phrase filters that dropped Spanish reporters, caps, rejection rules, a keyword matcher. The version of September 15 cost about $1 a person and gave Liam 91 sources.
- **September 16.** The journey settled. X accounts were dropped from monitoring and the positioning became the internet X does not show you. A founder-diagnostic pass put the company at step one: no reactions, no payers, talk to the five first.
- **September 17.** The working documents and scripts were deleted and the algorithm written down. An eight-part walk of the whole stack against the repo and the platforms' own documentation produced the facts now in sections 5 to 10. The plan critique gained a second Codex reader and quality control a third.
- **September 18.** Jev was restored and placed in code before Grok. A results page made the algorithm visible step by step, and looking at real runs did what reading plans had not: replies and threads were seen to be fragments and dropped; mentions were seen not to be credits; junk description lines were seen dragging scores down. Six outside models critiqued the algorithm with the full lab history as evidence, then proposed solutions under the owner's rulings; all of them and the assistant converged on one row per stream with a publisher, a focus and a written description. All 92 rows were re-read and rewritten into 76.
- **September 19.** The rebuilt pipeline ran end to end for 8 and 26 cents. The owner set the ten-source limit, defined the GitHub and Product Hunt digest after a read-only look at how Liam and Nihan actually post, and ended the experiments.

Rules the owner set for the assistant along the way: explain in chat, in plain terms, not by pointing at documents; never present an invention as a decision; no em dashes; skills are found, not installed, unless asked; do not zigzag; when something can be looked at, build the thing to look at, because seeing a real run settles in minutes what planning does not.
