# Acceptance criteria from the owner's words

His words come in two kinds, and they never mix:

- **The philosophy** (SKILL.md section 3, with the bar in section 1): fixed, for every page in every project. It changes only when he explicitly says "change this principle". His words: "The criteria don't have to adapt if we figure out what it is that produces the philosophy."
- **Per-screen criteria** (this file): what he says midway about one screen (for example, on October 2, that the feed must be readable without clicking and that websites and RSS feeds must be named separately). They refine how the philosophy is applied to that screen; they never override, loosen or replace it. A midway remark that seems to contradict the philosophy is read through it, and if it truly conflicts, ask him a yes or no question instead of choosing.

## Pattern

1. Re-read every message of his since the last accepted state, in order. Ask: what did he say that the last render ignored?
2. Write each point as one numbered criterion with his verbatim quote. Keep his phrasing; do not convert it into a measurement.
3. State in the brief: "Where research conflicts with these points, the points win." Research describes references; it does not decide what he wants.
4. Every direction must meet every criterion. Reviewers check renders against the list, not against their own taste.
5. When a criterion collides with a locked earlier decision of his, do not change it silently: ask a yes or no question beside the render.

## Worked example (Oparax, October 1 to 2, 2026)

These ten were written after the owner asked "Did you not pick up on everything I told you?" They produced the accepted round.

1. Variation: "there's not enough variation for me to comment on." Directions must look clearly different at a glance.
2. A visible accent, like Supabase's green: "There's a green tint there, and it still looks good. I don't see it in any of our four designs."
3. Several functional hues: "yellow, blue, green, and red elements, but in the relevant location, not as something random."
4. Soft depth and light: "the shadowing or the gradient colors, which are not aggressive gradients... gives a very polished feel."
5. A live product window: "this Linear card appears against the background, and it shows exactly what Linear is doing in real time."
6. Complexity shown neatly: "Complexity is still represented in a consistent design system, and it still looks good."
7. Animations in dark and light: "along with the react bits animations etc. How they will render not just with dark but with light mode too."
8. Imaginative directions inside his constraints: "show me a bunch of different directions in my constraints, but imaginative."
9. Hue that tells kinds of source apart, and a faint accent tint in the neutrals.
10. His palette family holds and dark on dark must still separate: "For some reason, Linear's dark on dark works."

The research that preceded them had concluded "color only as 6 to 14px dots, about 0.5 percent of the screen". That conclusion, applied literally, produced the renders he called lifeless. The criteria overrode it.
